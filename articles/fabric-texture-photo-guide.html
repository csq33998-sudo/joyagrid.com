Fabric

How to judge fabric and texture from photos

Photos cannot tell you everything, but they can reveal enough to avoid many weak buys. Texture, weight, shine, and drape all change how a piece feels in an outfit.

Fleece and cotton

Look for structure around cuffs, hem, hood, and collar. Very thin fleece can collapse in photos, while heavier cotton usually holds a cleaner shape.

Denim and washed fabric

Check whether the wash looks natural across seams and pockets. Uneven fading can be intentional, but harsh or patchy fading may look worse in person.

Nylon and technical fabric

Nylon jackets and bags can look glossy under warehouse lights. Compare several photos to see whether the shine is fabric finish or lighting.

Knitwear

Inspect ribbing, collar stretch, hem shape, and sleeve volume. Loose or warped knit edges can make a sweater look tired quickly.

Leather-like materials

Watch for plastic shine, uneven grain, and stiff folds. A subtle texture usually looks more wearable than a mirror-like surface.

Account for lighting and image processing

Direct flash exaggerates shine and surface texture, while soft studio light can hide both. Phone sharpening may make fleece look denser and compression can blur fine knit structure. Compare the same area across overview and close-up photos, especially near folds where light changes direction. If the color or sheen shifts dramatically between images, ask for a neutral-light photo beside a familiar reference rather than guessing which image is accurate.

Use drape as evidence of weight and structure

Look at how the fabric falls from a hanger, bends at the elbow, or gathers above a waistband. Deep, rounded folds often suggest more body; many sharp folds can indicate a thin or crisp fabric, although construction also matters. Check whether ribbing pulls the hem inward, whether a collar stands without support, and whether pocket edges distort the surrounding panel. These clues are useful for comparison, not a substitute for an actual fabric weight or composition.

Ask for photos that answer one question

Request a close-up of the outer face, the inside surface, a seam allowance, and the care or composition label when available. A backlit image can reveal thinness, while a gently scrunched section can show recovery and wrinkling. Do not infer fiber content, waterproofing, or durability from appearance alone. When the listing makes a performance claim, look for specifications or testing information that supports it.
